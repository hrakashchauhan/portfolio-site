The decision record

Jeevan Jhola, end to end — the case I'd walk you through in an interview.

✕ KILLED · ON PURPOSE

Founder · Apr – Jul 2025 · reusable-bag venture

Problem
Reusable bags lose to plastic for one reason: you have to remember them. Jeevan Jhola was built to remove the remembering — a recharge-and-return model where you pick a bag up where you are and hand it back where you end up.
Approach
I built the product and the website, recruited and led a team of interns, and planned the rollout. Before spending on a launch, I put the idea under pressure: a concept study, a mock survey, and user reviews — asking whether the support people voiced would survive their actual habits.
Decision
The evidence was consistent and unflattering. People agreed it was a good idea, then reached for whatever was already in their hand. The blocker was habit, not the product — and habit doesn't move because a founder wants it to. I shut it down before the launch spend instead of after.
Outcome
The venture ended while it was still cheap to be wrong. What the four months bought: proof I can take an idea from zero to a tested verdict — and act on the verdict when it goes against me.
Learning
Building was the easy part; deciding was the hard part. Since then I weight observed behaviour over stated intent, and I treat stopping as something to practise — not a failure to explain.

Exact counts — interns led, survey responses, reviews gathered — on request. Real numbers, not rounded ones.

06 /

How I work

  1. 01Clarity before speed.Most execution problems are clarity problems wearing a costume. Name the real thing first and half the chaos disappears.
  2. 02The detail is the strategy.You can't manage from the top what you've never touched at the bottom. Better to know how the stock reconciles than to have an opinion about it.
  3. 03Follow-up beats excitement.The person who closes the loop usually wins. It's unglamorous, which is exactly why it's rare.
  4. 04Test reality before building for it.People agree with a good idea and then do whatever's easiest — so I check what they actually do, not what they say, before committing resources.
  5. 05Knowing what to stop is a skill.Starting is easy. Quitting the right thing, on purpose, before it drains more than it's worth, is the harder and more useful decision.
